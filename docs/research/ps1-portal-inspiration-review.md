# PS1 portal inspiration review

Date reviewed: 30 September 2026

## Purpose and limits

This review compares the ten first-party pages supplied as references for the Royal Glass PS1 prototype. It focuses on audience, standard-versus-specific design routing, intake information, supporting documents, eligibility gates, follow-up, compliance language, PS1/PS3 distinction, calls to action, and UX/content patterns.

The pages are not equivalent products. Metro and UNEX expose real PS1 routing or generation journeys; Scenik exposes downloadable system PS1s plus a contact fallback; New Concept and 360 Glass mainly explain capability and funnel people into a quote or email conversation. Conclusions below distinguish those roles rather than treating every page as a form benchmark.

## Executive findings

1. **Route the applicant before asking for a long technical form.** Metro separates standard and specific design at the portal entrance: standard is for Metroview balustrades and pool fences, while specific design covers canopies, point-fixed glazing, glass floors, and custom balustrades. Its deeper guidance then routes specific work into vertical glazing, sloped glazing, balustrade, or glass-floor requests. ([Metro PS1 portal](https://metroglass.co.nz/resources/ps1), [Metro specific-design page](https://metroglass.co.nz/resources/ps1/specific-design))
2. **A “standard” path still needs hard eligibility gates.** Metro excludes waterproof-membrane penetrations and curved glass from its standard form, and its specific-balustrade path captures building height, structural design basis, wind pressure, fixing detail, fall height, system, rail, substrate, and drawings. ([Metro standard-design page](https://metroglass.co.nz/resources/ps1/standard-design), [Metro specific-design balustrade page](https://metroglass.co.nz/resources/ps1/specific-design/balustrade))
3. **Product-first selection works only when the catalogue is pre-engineered.** UNEX has users choose product category, product style, rail type, and fixing method before completing a short project/specifier form; the resulting PS1 downloads immediately. ([UNEX EziSpec guide](https://unex.co.nz/ezispec/), [UNEX technical portal](https://unex.co.nz/techportal/))
4. **The new Royal Glass prototype should combine guided simplicity with technical triage.** Keep Roxy's approachable eight-step application, but introduce a short early decision gate: known standard system versus needs checking/specific design. Do not expose the full existing generator's engineering options to an unsure homeowner.
5. **Save-and-return would be a meaningful competitive improvement.** Metro tells users to gather all information and finish in one session; its specific-design page says the RFI form does not automatically save. Royal Glass should preserve drafts and show a missing-information checklist. ([Metro standard-design page](https://metroglass.co.nz/resources/ps1/standard-design), [Metro specific-design balustrade page](https://metroglass.co.nz/resources/ps1/specific-design/balustrade))
6. **Clarify timing and document responsibility before submit.** Metro gives separate review and engineering timeframes; New Concept says producer statements should be raised at quoting stage; 360 Glass explains the whole estimate-to-install process. This reduces the chance that applicants expect an instant signed PS1 for non-standard work. ([Metro specific-design page](https://metroglass.co.nz/resources/ps1/specific-design), [New Concept PS1/PS3 page](https://newconcept.co.nz/about-us/ps1-ps3-certificate/), [360 Glass balustrades page](https://www.360glass.co.nz/residential/glass-balustrades))

## Detailed observations

### 1. Audience and level of technical language

Metro's detailed portals assume that the user can provide design inputs such as BCA, occupancy, wind zone/load, substrate, glass height, fixing section and drawings. UNEX explicitly targets architects, specifiers and developers, and its workflow starts from a known proprietary product combination. ([Metro standard-design page](https://metroglass.co.nz/resources/ps1/standard-design), [UNEX EziSpec](https://unex.co.nz/ezispec/))

New Concept and 360 Glass use homeowner-accessible language and allow the supplier to determine the right documentation or system from plans and a later site visit. ([New Concept PS1/PS3 page](https://newconcept.co.nz/about-us/ps1-ps3-certificate/), [360 Glass balustrades page](https://www.360glass.co.nz/residential/glass-balustrades))

**Implication for Royal Glass:** ask the applicant's role early and adapt help text. A designer/builder can receive technical fields immediately; a homeowner should be able to answer “not sure” and upload plans/photos without being forced to nominate wind pressure, fixing code, or glass build-up.

### 2. Standard versus specific design

Metro provides the clearest decision model. Standard is a bounded, tested design path. Specific design is needed when conditions fall outside it—for example a waterproof membrane, curved glass, building over 10 m, non-standard structural basis, engineered wind pressure, or a fixing/system not covered by the standard catalogue. ([Metro standard-design page](https://metroglass.co.nz/resources/ps1/standard-design), [Metro specific-design balustrade page](https://metroglass.co.nz/resources/ps1/specific-design/balustrade))

The overview also warns that a PS1 is not always the right compliance document: an acceptable solution such as NZS 4223 may instead support a design statement, while a specific PS1 can carry fees and requires an engineer. ([Metro overview](https://metroglass.co.nz/resources/ps1/overview))

UNEX's instant workflow is viable because it stays inside pre-engineered products and enumerated rail/fixing combinations. ([UNEX EziSpec](https://unex.co.nz/ezispec/))

**Implication for Royal Glass:** the prototype should not promise instant generation merely because the applicant selects a named system. It should classify the request as:

- **Likely standard design** — known Royal Glass system and supported conditions;
- **Needs technical checking** — applicant is unsure or information is missing;
- **Likely specific design** — a clear exception is present.

This classification should be presented as preliminary until staff review.

### 3. Intake fields worth adapting

Across Metro and UNEX, the recurring minimum dataset is:

- requester/applicant and document-recipient details;
- company/role and other project parties;
- project name, street address, and legal description where known;
- Building Consent Authority and consent/reference number where available;
- intended area/use: deck, balcony, stair, pool, void, etc.;
- occupancy/use category, building height, fall greater than 1 m;
- wind zone or design wind pressure;
- preferred system/style and fixing orientation;
- fixing substrate/supporting structure;
- barrier/glass height, lineal length, rail requirement, glass preference;
- waterproof membrane, curved/bespoke glass, and other special requirements;
- Auckland Council cover-sheet requirement where relevant.

These fields are evidenced by [Metro's standard checklist](https://metroglass.co.nz/resources/ps1/standard-design), [Metro's specific-balustrade checklist](https://metroglass.co.nz/resources/ps1/specific-design/balustrade), and the [UNEX technical portal form](https://unex.co.nz/techportal/).

The Royal Glass prototype should distinguish **required to submit** from **required before engineering can finish**. For example, a homeowner should be able to submit without knowing wind pressure, but the resulting summary must flag it as “Needs checking.”

### 4. Supporting documents

Metro's specific-design flow asks for plan, elevation, and fixing-detail/cross-section drawings, preferably supported by a wind map or site/building drawings when wind pressure must be calculated. It will only issue specific-design documents against architectural or construction drawings that specify Metro systems. ([Metro specific-design page](https://metroglass.co.nz/resources/ps1/specific-design), [Metro balustrade RFI](https://metroglass.co.nz/resources/ps1/specific-design/balustrade))

Metro's Point Fix page demonstrates why documents should be system-linked: it exposes design tables, installation instructions, elevations, rail/bracket details, and substrate-specific PDF/DWG files before the PS1 request. ([Metro Point Fix](https://metroglass.co.nz/products/balustrades-pool-fences/point-fix))

Scenik's calculator accepts plans, sketches or site photos and explains that they improve estimate accuracy—a useful low-friction pattern even though it is a pricing journey rather than PS1 intake. ([Scenik calculator](https://www.scenik.co.nz/calculator/))

**Implication for Royal Glass:** group uploads by purpose rather than offer one anonymous upload box:

- architectural plans;
- elevations/sections and fixing details;
- structural/wind information;
- consent or council RFI;
- site photos/sketches;
- other supporting material.

Show “recommended” or “required for specific design” beside each, and allow the staff review to request missing categories.

### 5. RFI and follow-up process

Metro uses **RFI** to mean the structured Request for Information submitted to its technical team, not only a later request sent back to the applicant. The specific-balustrade page describes the “specific design balustrade RFI” and warns that the form does not automatically save. ([Metro specific-design balustrade](https://metroglass.co.nz/resources/ps1/specific-design/balustrade))

The standard path uses a unique emailed link per project and tells users to quote their request reference if the completed document does not arrive within 1–5 working days. The specific path has a staged process: review, receipt of all information, acceptance of a compliance-document quote, then engineering. ([Metro standard-design page](https://metroglass.co.nz/resources/ps1/standard-design), [Metro specific-design page](https://metroglass.co.nz/resources/ps1/specific-design))

**Implication for Royal Glass:** in applicant-facing language, prefer “We need more information” over the unexplained acronym RFI. Keep “Council RFI” as a document/category when that is what the applicant has received. The prototype should show:

1. Submitted;
2. Technical review;
3. More information needed (if applicable);
4. Price/engineering acceptance (if specific design or paid work);
5. PS1 prepared and issued.

Draft saving and a secure resume link would improve materially on the competitor journey.

### 6. Legal and compliance wording

Metro's overview is careful about status: it calls PS1 a design-stage statement, says it does not issue retrospective PS1s, distinguishes acceptable solutions/design statements, notes that producer statements have no statutory status and BCA acceptance is discretionary, and says specific PS1s are engineer-issued. ([Metro overview](https://metroglass.co.nz/resources/ps1/overview), [Metro specific-design page](https://metroglass.co.nz/resources/ps1/specific-design))

Metro's published standard-request guide also requires the requester to declare that the information is accurate, that Metro has not sighted the drawings, and that architectural drawings will exactly match the selected PS1 details; it includes waiver/indemnity wording and confirmation of the standard structural compliance pathway. ([Metro Standard PS1 user guide PDF](https://assets.metroglass.co.nz/files/resources/ps1/metro-standard-ps1-user-guide.pdf))

New Concept distinguishes product/hardware design compliance from construction compliance and ties its documentation to actual supplied product rather than a generic template. ([New Concept PS1/PS3 page](https://newconcept.co.nz/about-us/ps1-ps3-certificate/))

**Implication for Royal Glass:** the prototype can show a plain-language acknowledgement, but production declarations, reliance wording, indemnities, and acceptance evidence require Royal Glass's engineer/legal approval. Do not copy competitor terms. A safe prototype acknowledgement is: “I confirm the information supplied is accurate to the best of my knowledge, and I understand Royal Glass will review it before confirming the appropriate compliance document.”

### 7. PS1 and PS3 distinction

Metro defines PS1 as design-stage documentation and PS3 as the installer's post-installation construction statement confirming that glass, hardware, fixing mechanism and substrate match the consented design/Building Code. It also notes that PS3 does not always depend on a prior PS1, but where design documentation exists, installation and PS3 must accord with it. ([Metro overview](https://metroglass.co.nz/resources/ps1/overview))

New Concept gives an especially clear customer explanation: PS1 covers hardware/product design, while PS3 covers installation work the company performed. It advises clients to raise both at quoting stage and identifies Auckland Council forms AC2322, AC2343 and AC2344. ([New Concept PS1/PS3 page](https://newconcept.co.nz/about-us/ps1-ps3-certificate/))

**Implication for Royal Glass:** keep the prototype focused on PS1 intake, but add a short “What about PS3?” note near the request decision. Ask whether Royal Glass will install the system only if that answer changes later service/PS3 handling; do not mix the two producer statements into one unexplained selection.

### 8. Calls to action and presentation patterns

- Metro's main portal uses three high-level cards and repeats a technical-support email, which is strong for routing but visually separates education from action. ([Metro PS1 portal](https://metroglass.co.nz/resources/ps1))
- UNEX uses a large visual product catalogue and makes the system/rail/fixing hierarchy explicit; it suits a specifier who already knows the product family. ([UNEX technical portal](https://unex.co.nz/techportal/))
- Metro's Point Fix page pairs visual appeal with a compact specification table and substrate-specific drawings; this is the strongest model for an individual system information card. ([Metro Point Fix](https://metroglass.co.nz/products/balustrades-pool-fences/point-fix))
- Scenik puts named system documents directly on one page and reserves a tiny form for exceptional requests. ([Scenik PS1 documents](https://www.scenik.co.nz/ps1-documents/))
- 360 Glass uses benefit-led headings, real project imagery, a five-stage process, and FAQs before a single quote CTA. It is a useful model for tone and reassurance, not technical intake. ([360 Glass balustrades](https://www.360glass.co.nz/residential/glass-balustrades))

## Recommended adaptations to the Royal Glass prototype

### Adopt now

1. **Add a short opening explainer and router.** “Do you need a standard Royal Glass system checked, a specific/custom design, or are you not sure?” Include one-line examples and preserve “Not sure.”
2. **Use an early technical gate.** Ask about application type, building height, fall greater than 1 m, wind information, substrate, waterproof membrane, curved/bespoke glass, and whether drawings specify a Royal Glass system.
3. **Show a preliminary route outcome.** “Likely standard,” “Needs checking,” or “Likely specific design,” with no claim of approval.
4. **Show named systems visually, but separate appearance from engineering.** Each card should have a photo/diagram, common use, side/base fixing, supported substrates, and an “I’m not sure” escape. Final system selection remains staff-controlled.
5. **Provide a before-you-start checklist.** Make it dynamic based on the route. Standard requests need less; specific requests should call out plans, elevations, sections/fixing details, and wind/structural evidence.
6. **Save drafts automatically and offer a secure resume link.** This directly addresses the one-session/no-save weakness visible in Metro's journey.
7. **Give transparent next steps and timing.** Separate initial review time from engineering/issue time and state when a quote or extra fee may be required.
8. **Use plain language first.** Display “More information needed” to applicants, with “RFI” as secondary terminology only where useful to staff or councils.
9. **Include a clean final summary.** Applicant, project, team, intended system, design conditions, uploaded document checklist, request outcome, declaration, and contact for the issued document.
10. **Provide a fallback contact path on every technical step.** Metro, UNEX, Scenik, New Concept and 360 Glass all preserve a human route rather than forcing every case through automation.

### Defer until rules are confirmed

- automatic PS1 generation;
- authoritative standard/specific classification rules;
- exact system/substrate/wind/fall/height combination matrix;
- engineering fees and service-level promises;
- final declarations, waivers or indemnity language;
- AC2322/AC2343/AC2344 production handling;
- PS3 issuance workflow;
- retrospective-request policy;
- document-retention and applicant-access periods.

These need confirmation from Royal Glass technical staff and, for legal wording, appropriate legal/engineering review.

## Proposed prototype content sequence

The existing eight-step concept can remain, with the competitor learnings inserted as follows:

1. **About you** — role, company, contact details, recipient of the PS1.
2. **Project team** — owner/developer, designer/architect, builder/installer; allow same-person shortcuts.
3. **Project** — project name/address, BCA, consent number, legal description if known, new/existing project, stage.
4. **What are you building?** — balustrade/pool barrier/application area; known system or “not sure”; visual system cards.
5. **Design conditions** — height, fall, wind, substrate, fixing orientation, membrane, curved/bespoke glass, rail preference; show live preliminary route.
6. **Documents** — purpose-labelled uploads plus a route-specific checklist.
7. **What do you need?** — price first, PS1/design review, help deciding; explain PS1 versus PS3 and likely next step.
8. **Review and submit** — readable application summary, missing-item warnings, approved acknowledgement text, processing expectations, secure resume/submission reference.

This keeps Roxy's guided applicant journey while incorporating the strongest technical and content patterns from Metro and UNEX and the clearer customer language used by New Concept and 360 Glass.
